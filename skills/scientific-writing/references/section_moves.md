# Section moves

Move-by-move templates. Every quoted sentence is attested in the Tier 1 corpus
(`trial_to_trial` 2026 Nat Commun, `tms_risk` 2025 preprint) unless marked
otherwise. Use the moves as a skeleton, never the sentences as a mad-lib — the
point is the shape of the argument, not the wording.

---

## Abstract

Eight sentences, give or take, in a fixed order. The Nature-family version
compresses moves 4–6; the preprint version expands them.

1. **The phenomenon, with the central construct defined by em-dash apposition,
   and the incumbent view named.**
   > "Risk attitudes—the willingness to accept uncertainty for larger potential
   > rewards—are often treated as a stable individual trait, yet people's risky
   > choices change markedly across contexts and even across repetitions of
   > identical decisions."

   The preprint variant splits this into two sentences and names the incumbent's
   anatomy: "Risk preferences have traditionally been considered as stable traits
   that reflect subjective-valuation processes in prefrontal areas."

2. **The gap, as a question the correlational evidence leaves open.**
   > "However, this correlational relationship leaves open the question whether
   > parietal magnitude representations in fact causally underlie choice."

3. **"Here we show / Here, we provide" — the claim, with the methods named in the
   same sentence.**
   > "Here we show, using modelling and neuroimaging, that such rapid changes
   > emerge from Bayesian inference on noisy magnitude representations in
   > parietal cortex."
   > "Here, we provide direct evidence for such a key causal role of parietal
   > magnitude representations in economic choice, using continuous theta-burst
   > transcranial magnetic stimulation (cTBS), combined with functional MRI
   > (fMRI) and numerical population receptive field (nPRF) modeling."

4. **The design, in one sentence, with what makes it work.**
   > "Participants underwent fMRI while choosing between safe and risky options
   > that are either held in working memory or shown on screen."

5. **The behavioural result, stated as a mechanism rather than a statistic.**
   > "Options held in working memory are noisier and more prone to
   > central-tendency biases, so relatively small payoffs are overestimated and
   > large payoffs underestimated, shifting choices between risk-seeking and risk
   > aversion."

6. **The modelling result.** "A computational model of payoff perception captures
   these effects mechanistically."

7. **The neural result.** "Numerical population-receptive-field modelling shows
   that when intraparietal payoff representations are noisier, choices are less
   consistent and less risk-neutral."

8. **The scoped conclusion, opened with `Thus,` or `In conclusion,`, with the
   hedge in the verb.**
   > "Thus, risk attitudes and their contextual variability **can be** rooted in
   > perceptual inference on parietal magnitude representations."

No statistics in the abstract except sample size when the design is the point
("n=35; within-subject design").

## Introduction

Five moves, ending in a triple.

### Move 1 — the phenomenon, instantiated in everyday life, in threes

Lab first, then life, then the trait framing:

> "In lab experiments, some people will forgo a lottery with a larger expected
> value for a substantially lower but certain payout, whereas others will choose
> the lottery. Relatedly, in everyday life, some people are more willing than
> others to become self-employed, engage in new relationships, or to invest money
> in the stock market."

The preprint version uses concrete instruments (stocks vs bonds, car insurance
deductibles) and then lands the trait framing as an analogy with a deliberately
mundane preference:

> "Many economic theorists view such risk attitudes as an individual trait: just
> as some people particularly dislike electronic music, others may particularly
> dislike taking risks."

### Move 2 — the incumbent account, named by field and given its formal definition

> "Neoclassical economic theories describe such individual differences in
> behaviour using the concept of individual risk preferences: An individual would
> be risk-averse if they prefer to obtain for sure the expected value of a risky
> prospect (its gain multiplied by its probability) to realising the risky
> prospect itself, risk-seeking if they prefer the risky prospect itself to
> obtaining its expected value for sure, and risk-neutral if they are indifferent
> between these options."

Then state why the incumbent matters practically — the stakes of being wrong:

> "Thus, measuring overt risk attitudes in laboratory choices is the standard way
> to infer underlying risk preferences, which is seen as key for any statements,
> recommendations, or interventions designed to help people take decisions under
> uncertainty…"

### Move 3 — the empirical crack

Opened with a contrastive: "Empirical data reveal, however, that individual risk
attitudes are highly unstable." Then the specific instabilities, each cited:
context, order, repetition. Close by naming what the incumbent's patches
(Random Utility Theory, response noise) do *not* explain:

> "Although these theories alleviate the problem of choice inconsistency, they do
> not specifically address the question of where this randomness comes from and
> generally do not explain why risk attitudes are so dependent on context."

### Move 4 — the imported mechanism, with its normative justification

Name the source field explicitly ("By contrast, numerous findings in perceptual
neuroscience suggest…"), give the mechanism, and — this is the characteristic
step — say why the bias is *optimal*:

> "Note that, on average, such biases are beneficial because they reduce the
> average perceptual error, by employing prior information about the likely
> causes of sensory signals in case these are noisy and therefore unreliable."

Then the consequence for the phenomenon, ending on the reframe's punchline:

> "This suggests that two people might in fact be similarly risk-neutral, but may
> show substantially different risk attitudes in their choices simply because they
> differ in how precisely and thus unbiased they can perceive a risky choice
> problem."

Cite your own prior work here as scaffolding, not as credit: "We have recently
provided initial neuroscientific evidence for such a perceptual account, by
showing that…"

### Move 5 — the triple close

**5a. The enumerated open questions.** Each maps to a Results subsection:

> "First, what determines whether individuals show categorically distinct risk
> attitudes such as risk-seeking, risk-neutral, and risk-averse behaviour? …
> Second, risk attitudes appear highly sensitive to contexts… Can we
> mechanistically account for the influence of context on economic choice…?
> Finally… Where do these apparent fluctuations in risk attitudes come from?"

**5b. "To address these questions, we introduce…"** — the model or method, with
the feature that distinguishes it from what exists:

> "The crucial feature compared to earlier models is that the acuity with which
> the payoffs of different choice options are represented can dynamically change
> as a function of exogenous (i.e., choice architecture) or endogenous (i.e.,
> neural/physiological state) context."

**5c. The roadmap paragraph.** Explicit, first-person, future tense:

> "In the following, we will first describe the risky choice paradigm we
> developed to modulate the acuity with which choice options are represented in
> the brain, by manipulating the order in which they are presented."

Fold in the justification of any unusual stimulus choice here, pre-empting the
reviewer: dot clouds rather than numerals, why that is defensible, and that a
symbolic control experiment was run.

**5d. The preview paragraph.** Either "Here, we show that…" restating all three
findings in order, or, in the preprint, literally flagged:

> "Previewing our results, stimulation reduced the fidelity of parietal tuning to
> the presented payoff magnitudes. Moreover, we found reduced choice consistency
> and increased risk-seeking behavior, but only for trials where…"

Close on the broad implication, which the Discussion will later cash out:

> "Together, these results provide critical insights into how neurocognitive
> representations of payoff magnitudes, localized in the parietal ANS, shape
> economic decisions and why people sometimes exhibit more or less risk-averse
> behavior."

## Results

### Headings

Declarative, full predicate, 8–15 words. Attested:

> Experimental paradigm
> Different noise contexts shift risk attitudes as predicted by Bayesian inference on noisy payoff representations
> The Perception and Memory-based Choice Model (PMCM)
> Decoded payoff representations in parietal cortex predict noisiness and bias in choice
> Choices in symbolic presentation format also reveal shifts in risk attitude as a result of order and stake size
> Decreased amplitudes of numerically-tuned parietal cortex after cTBS
> Shifts in risk preference are driven by increased noise in small- but not large-magnitude representations

Note the two exceptions: a bare "Experimental paradigm" opener and a bare model
name are allowed, because neither makes a claim. Everything that reports a
finding states it.

### Within a subsection

1. **Where we are in the argument.**
   > "Having established that individual risk attitudes and changes in those
   > attitudes across different choice contexts can be explained by our Bayesian
   > perceptual account of risky choice, we addressed the final question of our
   > study: Where do the trial-to-trial fluctuations in risk attitudes come from?"

2. **The prediction, derived, before any data.**
   > "Thus, taking a perceptual Bayesian perspective, we predicted that the
   > options that were presented first should be more sensitive to the central
   > tendency (CT) bias, resulting in overestimation of relatively small payoffs
   > and underestimation of relatively large payoffs."

3. **Why this analysis can test it** — the measurement-model justification, which
   is never skipped:
   > "Critically, this model disentangles choice consistency from preference,
   > avoiding the confounds inherent in raw choice proportions."

4. **Hypotheses broken down before testing**, when there are two:
   > "To test this hypothesis carefully, we broke it down to two subhypotheses: On
   > trials where neural payoff representations are particularly noisy, choice
   > behaviour should (1) become less consistent and (2) more biased towards prior
   > beliefs. In the PMCM, both effects can jointly be explained by an increase in
   > noise. However, we aimed to first test both hypotheses separately."

5. **Sanity checks before the result of interest.** Decoder calibration,
   split-half reliability, parameter recovery — reported in the Results with
   numbers, not deferred to supplements.
   > "Before linking neural uncertainty to behaviour, we first assessed the acuity
   > of the decoder."

6. **The result, opened with the prediction-confirmation flag.**
   > "In line with our predictions, our psychophysical modelling showed a strong
   > interaction effect between order and overall stake size on RNP."
   > "In line with our expectations, the psychophysical slope was substantially
   > shallower for trials with a higher decoded neural uncertainty
   > (pBayesian = 0.046 for the 3T data set; pBayesian = 0.024 for the 7T data set)."

7. **The closing summary, flagged.**
   > "In sum, our experimental paradigm and the PMCM can mechanistically
   > characterise how a common and relevant context effect … impacts the risk
   > attitude of the participants."
   > "Taking stock, the key finding of this additional experiment is that the
   > order in which options are presented shapes risk attitudes, and this effect
   > interacts with overall stake size—even when using more traditional, symbolic
   > stimuli."
   > "In summary, the analysis of choice behavior using psychophysical modeling
   > shows a robust effect of parietal cTBS on both choice consistency and average
   > risk preference…"

8. **The forward-looking transition** to the next subsection.
   > "We now turn to more mechanistic computational models of choice to better
   > understand this empirical pattern."

### Objections inside Results

Model complexity is defended where it arises, not in the Discussion:

> "One question about our model might be whether it is unnecessarily complex, as
> it contains relatively many parameters to fit the data. We addressed this
> question with both qualitative predictive checks and quantitative model
> comparison techniques, testing whether both dynamic noise and different priors
> for risky/safe options are strictly necessary to explain the empirical data."

And the model-recovery answer to "is your comparison even powered":

> "To make sure that our model comparison approach was sufficiently powerful, we
> also simulated 100 data sets using the 5 different models and used the same
> technique to estimate which was the generating model. When the PMCM was the
> generating model, it was correctly identified 100/100 times."

### Surprises are reported, not smoothed

> "However, surprisingly, a smaller number of participants (5/58) robustly
> (pBayesian < 0.05) showed more noise for the 2nd option than the 1st option,
> contrary to a memory noise effect. We believe these results might reflect
> differences in attentional strategies, which could be investigated in future
> modelling work using eye tracking and/or choice-specific noise manipulations."

Note the shape: the anomaly, its size, that it contradicts the account, a
speculative explanation flagged as speculation ("We believe… might"), and a
concrete way to settle it.

## Discussion

1. **Restate the problem and the contribution**, opening on the unresolved
   question rather than on the result.
   > "Overt risk attitudes—and the latent underlying risk preferences—play a
   > central role in many aspects of our behaviour, but we still don't completely
   > understand where they come from and why they change across different contexts
   > or even repetitions of identical choices. Here, we provide modelling and
   > neurobiological evidence that…"

2. **Numbered implications**, each its own paragraph, each naming whose problem
   it is.
   > "Our results have important implications for both theoretical and empirical
   > work on risk attitudes. First, in line with other recent work, they defy the
   > conventional economic wisdom that risk attitudes are stable…"
   > "A second key implication of our study—congruent with other recent work—is
   > that a large part of inter- and intra-individual variability in risky choice
   > behaviour can be explained by fluctuations in basic perceptual neurocognitive
   > representations, over and above any possible differences in subjective
   > valuation of the choice options."
   > "Our work also has implications for existing neuroeconomic studies and opens
   > up several research directions. First, … Second, …"

3. **The distinction from the alternative account, with its own limit
   acknowledged in the same sentence.**
   > "While this evidently does not prove that subjective valuation is not
   > involved in risky choices, it still suggests that perception may play an
   > important role and needs to be accounted for explicitly when researchers
   > attempt to measure risk preferences, at least in the small-stake laboratory
   > experiments widely used in the field."

4. **Objections, conceded then bounded.** Two attested templates:
   > "One might argue that this choice context may induce effects in a purely
   > 'low-level perceptual' manner that should be absent for choices with more
   > common Arabic numerals. However, there is ample evidence that…"
   > "A related critique of Bayesian theories, and potentially the PMCM, is that
   > they are not mechanistic models of neural function but rather prescriptive
   > models of information processing. Nevertheless, the PMCM does give us an
   > increased understanding of the mechanism by which…"

   Flexibility of a Bayesian model gets conceded outright before being answered:
   > "Because the mean and spread of these priors are free parameters, the PMCM is
   > indeed a relatively flexible model. However, both formal model comparison and
   > careful qualitative inspection of the empirical data show that less flexible
   > models of risky choice … can simply not account for the qualitative
   > behavioural patterns in our data. Having said that, future work should
   > validate whether…"

5. **Failed attempts are disclosed.**
   > "After some initial attempts at including such a learning mechanism in our
   > model, we believe that the choice paradigm we employed here is not suitable
   > for such questions, because the amount of information present in a single
   > accept/reject decision is limited."

6. **Future work as a design.** Name the manipulation, the measure, the paradigm.
   > "Future studies attempting to dissociate valuation and perception should
   > systematically manipulate both sets of processes. Valuation may be
   > experimentally manipulated by changing objective choice properties and
   > comparing qualitatively different decision-makers, while perception may be
   > influenced by orthogonally manipulating the acuity of the choice-relevant
   > information."

7. **The contribution list**, often placed late and explicitly counted:
   > "Our work aligns with a recent body of research that conceptualises economic
   > choice idiosyncrasies as rational responses to cognitive capacity constraints
   > and makes three contributions to this literature. First, … Second, … Third, …"

8. **The reach ladder** — mechanism, then domain, then policy or clinic, each with
   a real referent rather than a gesture. Gambling arousal and unrealistic
   beliefs; personal banking eliciting client risk preferences; psychiatric
   symptom characterisation.

9. **"To conclude, …"** — one paragraph, restating the mechanism, the tool it
   gives other researchers, and the scoped generalisation. Ends on what the field
   should now do differently, not on a summary.
   > "More generally, our study demonstrates that attempts to elicit (risk)
   > preferences need to consider the substantial variability in responses and
   > should be mindful of potential confounds like presentation (or fixation)
   > order and the general range of options offered during an experiment."
